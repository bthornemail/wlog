## Alternative units

See also: [Measuring angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle#Measuring_angles "Angle")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/Degree-Radian_Conversion.svg/330px-Degree-Radian_Conversion.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Degree-Radian_Conversion.svg)

A chart to convert between degrees and radians

In most [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") work beyond practical geometry, angles are typically measured in [radians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radian "Radian") rather than degrees. This is for a variety of reasons; for example, the [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function "Trigonometric function") have simpler and more "natural" properties when their arguments are expressed in radians. These considerations outweigh the convenient divisibility of the number 360. One complete [turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(geometry\) "Turn (geometry)") (360°) is equal to 2_[π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi")_ radians, so 180° is equal to π radians, or equivalently, the degree is a [mathematical constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_constant "Mathematical constant"): 1° = π⁄180.

One [turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(geometry\) "Turn (geometry)") (corresponding to a cycle or revolution) is equal to 360°.

With the invention of the [metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_system "Metric system"), based on powers of ten, there was an attempt to replace degrees by decimal "degrees" in France and nearby countries,[[note 3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_\(angle\)#cite_note-17) where the number in a right angle is equal to 100 gon with 400 gon in a full circle (1° = 10⁄9 gon). This was called _grade (nouveau)_ or _[grad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grad_\(angle\) "Grad (angle)")_. Due to confusion with the existing term _grad(e)_ in some northern European countries (meaning a standard degree, ⁠1/360⁠ of a turn), the new unit was called _Neugrad_ in [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language") (whereas the "old" degree was referred to as _Altgrad_), likewise _nygrad_ in [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language "Danish language"), [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language "Swedish language") and [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language "Norwegian language") (also _gradian_), and _nýgráða_ in [Icelandic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_language "Icelandic language"). To end the confusion, the name _gon_ was later adopted for the new unit. Although this idea of metrification was abandoned by Napoleon, grades continued to be used in several fields and many [scientific calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_calculator "Scientific calculator") support them. Decigrades (1⁄4000) were used with French artillery sights in World War I.

An [angular mil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_mil "Angular mil"), which is most used in military applications, has at least three specific variants, ranging from 1⁄6400 to 1⁄6000. It is approximately equal to one [milliradian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milliradian "Milliradian") (c. 1⁄6,283). A mil measuring 1⁄6000 of a revolution originated in the [imperial Russian army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Russian_Army "Imperial Russian Army"), where an equilateral chord was divided into tenths to give a circle of 600 units. This may be seen on a lining plane (an early device for aiming [indirect fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indirect_fire "Indirect fire") artillery) dating from about 1900 in the [St. Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Petersburg "St. Petersburg") Museum of Artillery.

## Advantages and disadvantages

Each quadrant is assigned a range of 100 gon, which eases recognition of the four quadrants, as well as arithmetic involving perpendicular or opposite angles.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|0°|=|0 gradians|
|90°|=|100 gradians|
|180°|=|200 gradians|
|270°|=|300 gradians|
|360°|=|400 gradians|

One advantage of this unit is that right angles to a given angle are easily determined. If one is sighting down a compass course of 117 gon, the direction to one's left is 17 gon, to one's right 217 gon, and behind one 317 gon. A disadvantage is that the common angles of 30° and 60° in geometry must be expressed in fractions (as ⁠33+1/3⁠ gon and ⁠66+2/3⁠ gon respectively).

## Relation to the metre 

Further information: [History of the metre § Meridional definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_metre#Meridional_definition "History of the metre")

See also: [Arc length § Great circles on Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_length#Great_circles_on_Earth "Arc length"), and [Nautical mile § Similar definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautical_mile#Similar_definitions "Nautical mile")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/14/Kilometre_definition.svg/250px-Kilometre_definition.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kilometre_definition.svg)

An early definition of the [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre "Metre") was one ten-millionth of the distance from the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole "North Pole") to the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator "Equator"), measured along a [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_\(geography\) "Meridian (geography)") through [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris").

In the 18th century, the [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre "Metre") was defined as the 10-millionth part of a [quarter meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_meridian "Quarter meridian"). Thus, 1 gon corresponds to an [arc length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_length "Arc length") along the Earth's surface of approximately 100 kilometres; 1 centigon to 1 kilometre; 10 microgons to 1 metre.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradian#cite_note-26) (The [metre has been redefined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre#History_of_definition "Metre") with increasing precision since then.)

The **steradian** (symbol: **sr**) or **square radian**[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steradian#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steradian#cite_note-2) is the unit of [solid angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_angle "Solid angle") in the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI). It is used in [three-dimensional geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_geometry "Three-dimensional geometry"), and is analogous to the [radian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radian "Radian"), which quantifies [planar angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planar_angle "Planar angle"). A solid angle in the form of a [circular cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_cone "Circular cone") can be projected onto a [sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere "Sphere") from its centre, delineating a [spherical cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_cap "Spherical cap") where the cone intersects the sphere. The magnitude of the solid angle expressed in steradians is defined as the quotient of the surface area of the spherical cap and the square of the sphere's radius. This is analogous to the way a plane angle projected onto a circle delineates a [circular arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_arc "Circular arc") on the circumference, whose length is proportional to the angle. Steradians can be used to measure a solid angle of any projected shape. The solid angle subtended is the same as that of a cone with the same projected area. A solid angle of one steradian subtends a [cone aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_aperture "Cone aperture") of approximately 1.144 radians or 65.54 degrees.

The **turn** (symbol **tr** or **pla**) is a unit of [plane angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_angle "Plane angle") measurement that is the measure of a [complete angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_angle "Complete angle")—the angle [subtended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtended_angle "Subtended angle") by a complete [circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle "Circle") at its center. One turn is equal to 2[_π_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi") [radians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radian "Radian"), 360 [degrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_\(angle\) "Degree (angle)") or 400 [gradians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradian "Gradian"). As an [angular unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_unit "Angular unit"), one turn also corresponds to one **cycle** (symbol **cyc** or **c**)[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Fitzpatrick_2021-1) or to one **revolution** (symbol **rev** or **r**).[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-IET_2016-2) Common related [units of frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency#Unit "Frequency") are _[cycles per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycles_per_second "Cycles per second")_ (cps) and _[revolutions per minute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_per_minute "Revolutions per minute")_ (rpm). The angular unit of the turn is useful in connection with, among other things, [electromagnetic coils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil "Electromagnetic coil") (e.g., [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer "Transformer")), rotating objects, and the [winding number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winding_number "Winding number") of curves. Divisions of a turn include the half-turn and quarter-turn, spanning a [straight angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle#Individual_angles "Angle") and a [right angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_angle "Right angle"), respectively; [metric prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefixes "Metric prefixes") can also be used as in, e.g., centiturns (ctr), milliturns (mtr), etc.

In the [ISQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Quantities "International System of Quantities"), an arbitrary "number of turns" (also known as "number of revolutions" or "number of cycles") is formalized as a [dimensionless quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantity "Dimensionless quantity") called _**rotation**_, defined as the [ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio "Ratio") of a given angle and a full turn. It is represented by the symbol _N_. (See [below](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#In_the_ISQ/SI) for the formula.)

Because one turn is 2π![{\displaystyle 2\pi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/73efd1f6493490b058097060a572606d2c550a06) radians, some have proposed representing 2π![{\displaystyle 2\pi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/73efd1f6493490b058097060a572606d2c550a06) with the single letter [𝜏 (tau)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_\(mathematics\) "Tau (mathematics)").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-3)

## Unit symbols

There are several unit symbols for the turn.


---


Many angle units are defined as a division of the turn. For example, the [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_\(angle\) "Degree (angle)") is defined such that one turn is 360 degrees.

Using [metric prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix), the turn can be divided in 100 centiturns or 1000 milliturns, with each milliturn corresponding to an [angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle "Angle") of 0.36°, which can also be written as [21′ 36″](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_and_second_of_arc "Minute and second of arc").[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Hoyle_1962-14)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Klein_2012-15) A [protractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protractor "Protractor") divided in centiturns is normally called a "[percentage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percentage "Percentage") protractor". While percentage protractors have existed since 1922,[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Croxton_1992-16) the terms centiturns, milliturns and microturns were introduced much later by the British astronomer [Fred Hoyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Hoyle "Fred Hoyle") in 1962.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Hoyle_1962-14)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Klein_2012-15) Some measurement devices for artillery and [satellite watching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_watching "Satellite watching") carry milliturn scales.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Schiffner_1965-17)[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Hayes_1975-18)

[Binary fractions of a turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_angular_measurement "Binary angular measurement") are also used. Sailors have traditionally divided a turn into 32 [compass points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Points_of_the_compass "Points of the compass"), which implicitly have an angular separation of ⁠1/32⁠ turn. The _binary degree_, also known as the _[binary radian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_radian "Binary radian")_ (or _brad_), is ⁠1/256⁠ turn.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Savage_2007-19) The binary degree is used in computing so that an angle can be represented to the maximum possible precision in a single [byte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte "Byte"). Other measures of angle used in computing may be based on dividing one whole turn into 2_n_ equal parts for other values of n.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_\(angle\)#cite_note-Hargreaves_2010-20)

### Metric units

#### Mass

The units [kilogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilogram "Kilogram"), [gram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram "Gram"), [milligram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milligram "Milligram"), microgram, and smaller are commonly used for measurement of [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass "Mass"). However, megagram, gigagram, and larger are rarely used; [tonnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonne "Tonne") (and kilotonnes, megatonnes, etc.) or [scientific notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_notation "Scientific notation") are used instead. The megagram does not share the risk of confusion that the tonne has with other units with the name "ton".

The kilogram is the only coherent unit of the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units "International System of Units") that includes a metric prefix.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix#cite_note-20): 144 

#### Volume

The [litre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litre "Litre") (equal to a cubic decimetre), millilitre (equal to a cubic centimetre), microlitre, and smaller are common. In Europe, the centilitre is often used for liquids (e.g. bottles or servings of wine), and the decilitre is used less frequently. Bulk agricultural products, such as grain, beer and wine, often use the hectolitre (100 litres).[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix#cite_note-21)

Larger volumes are usually denoted in kilolitres, megalitres or gigalitres, or else in cubic metres (1 cubic metre = 1 kilolitre) or cubic kilometres (1 cubic kilometre = 1 teralitre). For scientific (other than medical) purposes, the SI unit of cubic metre is usually used, with [scientific notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_notation "Scientific notation") rather than prefixes.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

#### Length

The kilometre, metre, centimetre, millimetre, and smaller units are common. The decimetre is rarely used. The micrometre is often referred to by the older non-SI name _[micron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrometre#SI_standardization "Micrometre")_, which is officially deprecated. In some fields, such as [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"), the [ångström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angstrom "Angstrom") (0.1 nm) has been used commonly instead of the nanometre. The [femtometre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femtometre "Femtometre"), used mainly in particle physics, is sometimes called a [fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_\(unit\) "Fermi (unit)"). For large scales, megametre, gigametre, and larger are rarely used. Instead, ad hoc non-metric units are used, such as the [solar radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_radius "Solar radius"), [astronomical units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit "Astronomical unit"), [light years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_year "Light year"), and [parsecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsec "Parsec"), and less commonly large multiples (e.g. millions) of kilometres; the astronomical unit is mentioned in the SI standards as an accepted non-SI unit.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix#cite_note-22)

#### Time

See also: [Metric time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_time "Metric time") and [Orders of magnitude (time)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude_\(time\) "Orders of magnitude (time)")

Prefixes for the SI standard unit [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second "Second") are most commonly encountered for quantities less than one second. For larger quantities, the system of [minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute "Minute") (60 seconds), [hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hour "Hour") (60 minutes) and [days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day "Day") (24 hours) is [accepted for use with the SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-SI_units_mentioned_in_the_SI "Non-SI units mentioned in the SI") and more commonly used. When speaking of spans of time, the length of the day is usually standardised to 86400 seconds so as not to create issues with the irregular [leap second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_second "Leap second").[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix#cite_note-23)

Larger multiples of the second such as kiloseconds and megaseconds are occasionally encountered in scientific contexts, but are seldom used in common parlance. For long-scale scientific work, particularly in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), the [Julian year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_year_\(astronomy\) "Julian year (astronomy)") or _annum_ (a) is a standardised variant of the [year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year "Year"), equal to exactly 31557600 seconds (⁠365+ 1 /4⁠ days). The unit is so named because it was the average length of a year in the [Julian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_calendar "Julian calendar"). Long time periods are then expressed by using metric prefixes with the annum, such as megaannum (Ma) or [gigaannum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigaannum "Gigaannum") (Ga).[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

#### Angle

The SI unit of angle is the [radian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radian "Radian"), but [degrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_\(angle\) "Degree (angle)"), as well as [arc-minutes and arc-seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutes_and_seconds_of_arc "Minutes and seconds of arc"), see some scientific use in fields such as astronomy.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix#cite_note-24)

#### Temperature

Common practice does not typically use the flexibility allowed by official policy in the case of the degree Celsius (°C). NIST states:[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix#cite_note-Special_Publication_811-25) "Prefix symbols may be used with the unit symbol °C and prefix names may be used with the unit name _degree Celsius_. For example, 12 m°C (12 millidegrees Celsius) is acceptable." In practice, it is more common for prefixes to be used with the [kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin "Kelvin") when it is desirable to denote extremely large or small absolute temperatures or temperature differences. Thus, temperatures of star interiors may be given with the unit of MK (megakelvin), and molecular cooling may be given with the unit mK (millikelvin).[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

#### Energy

In use the [joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule "Joule") and kilojoule are common, with larger multiples seen in limited contexts. In addition, the [kilowatt-hour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt-hour "Kilowatt-hour"), a composite unit formed from the [kilowatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt#Kilowatt "Watt") and hour, is often used for electrical energy; other multiples can be formed by modifying the prefix of watt (e.g. terawatt-hour).[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Several definitions exist for the non-SI unit [calorie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorie "Calorie"). Distinguished are gram calories and kilogram calories. One kilogram calorie, which equals one thousand gram calories, often appears capitalised and without a prefix (i.e. _Cal_) when referring to "[dietary calories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorie "Calorie")" in food.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix#cite_note-26) It is common to apply metric prefixes to the gram calorie, but not to the kilogram calorie: thus, 1 kcal = 1000 cal = 1 Cal.

The **Sator Square** (also called the **Rotas-Sator Square** or the **Templar Magic Square**) is a two-dimensional [acrostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrostic "Acrostic") class of [word square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_square "Word square") containing a five-word [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") [palindrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palindrome "Palindrome").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1) The earliest squares were found at Roman-era sites and were all in ROTAS form (i.e. where the top line is "ROTAS" and not "SATOR"), with the earliest discovery at [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii "Pompeii") (and dating from before the [earthquake of AD 62](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AD_62_Pompeii_earthquake "AD 62 Pompeii earthquake")).[[a]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-PS-2) The earliest square with Christian-associated imagery dates from the sixth century.[[b]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Coptic-3) By the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), Sator squares were to be found in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"), [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor"), and in [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Fishwick-4) In 2022, the _[Encyclopedia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia_Britannica "Encyclopedia Britannica")_ called it "the most familiar lettered square in the Western world".[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-EB-5)

A significant volume of academic research has been published on the square, but after more than a century, there is no consensus on its origin and meaning.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-RB-6)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-ENC-7) The emergence of the "Paternoster theory" in 1926 led to a brief consensus among academics that the square was created by early Christians, but the subsequent discoveries at Pompeii led many academics to believe that the square was more likely created as a Roman word puzzle (per the [Roma-Amor puzzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Venus_and_Roma#Architecture "Temple of Venus and Roma")), which was later adopted by Christians. This origin theory, however, fails to explain how a Roman word puzzle became such a powerful religious and magical medieval symbol. It has instead been argued that the square was created in its ROTAS form as a Jewish symbol, embedded with cryptic religious symbolism, which was later adopted in its SATOR form by Christians.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Fishwick-4)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Baines-8) Other less-supported academic origin theories include a [Pythagorean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreanism "Pythagoreanism") or [Stoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism "Stoicism") puzzle, a [Gnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnostic "Gnostic") or [Orphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orphism_\(religion\) "Orphism (religion)") or Italian pagan [amulet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amulet "Amulet"), a cryptic [Mithraic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithraic "Mithraic") or [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_people "Semitic people") numerology charm, or that it was a device for assessing wind direction.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1)

The square has long associations with magical powers throughout its history (and even up to the 19th century in North and South America), including a perceived ability to extinguish fires, particularly in Germany. The square appears in early and late medieval medical textbooks such as the [Trotula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trotula "Trotula"), and was employed as a medieval cure for many ailments, particularly for dog bites and [rabies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabies "Rabies"), as well as for insanity, and relief during childbirth.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Fishwick-4)

It has featured in a diverse range of contemporary artworks including fiction books, paintings, musical scores, and films,[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-ENC-7) and most notably in [Christopher Nolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Nolan "Christopher Nolan")'s 2020 film _[Tenet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenet "Tenet")_.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-vox-9) In 2020, _[The Daily Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Telegraph "The Daily Telegraph")_ called it "one of the closest things the classical world had to a [meme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meme "Meme")".[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-DT-10)

## Description and naming

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4b/Enigmatic_inscription_of_St_Peter_ad_Oratorium_400.jpg/250px-Enigmatic_inscription_of_St_Peter_ad_Oratorium_400.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Enigmatic_inscription_of_St_Peter_ad_Oratorium_400.jpg)

Sator square (in ROTAS form) on the eighth-century facade of [Abbey of St. Peter ad Oratorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Pietro_ad_Oratorium_Abbey "San Pietro ad Oratorium Abbey") in Italy

The Sator square is arranged as a 5 × 5 grid consisting of five 5-letter words, thus totaling 25 characters. It uses 8 different Latin letters: 5 consonants (S, T, R, P, N) and 3 vowels (A, E, O). In some versions, the vertical and horizontal lines of the grid are also drawn, but in many cases, there are no such lines. The square is described as a two-dimensional [palindrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palindrome "Palindrome"), or [word square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_square "Word square"), which is a particular class of a [double acrostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrostic "Acrostic").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-EB-5)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-11)

The square comes in two forms: ROTAS (left, below), and SATOR (right, below):[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Fishwick-4)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Baines-8)

The earliest Roman-era versions of the square have the word ROTAS as the top line (called a ROTAS-form square, left above), but the inverted version with SATOR in the top line became more dominant from early medieval times (called a SATOR-form square, right above).[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1) Some academics call it a Rotas-Sator Square,[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Fishwick-4)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Baines-8) and some of them refer to the object as a [rebus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebus "Rebus"),[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-:0-12) or a [magic square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_square "Magic square").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-Fishwick-4) Since medieval times, it has also been known as a Templar Magic Square.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-MRS-1)[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sator_Square#cite_note-13)
