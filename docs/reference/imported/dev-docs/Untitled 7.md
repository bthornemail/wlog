In [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics "Computer graphics"), a **sprite** is a [two-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_\(mathematics\) "Plane (mathematics)") [bitmap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitmap "Bitmap") that is integrated into a larger scene, most often in a 2D [video game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game "Video game"). Originally, the term _sprite_ referred to fixed-sized objects composited together, by hardware, with a background.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprite_\(computer_graphics\)#cite_note-dadgum-1) Use of the term has since become more general.

Hardware composition of sprites occurs as each [scan line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scan_line "Scan line") is prepared for the video output device, such as a [cathode-ray tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode-ray_tube "Cathode-ray tube"), without involvement of the main [CPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_processing_unit "Central processing unit") and without the need for a full-screen [frame buffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_buffer "Frame buffer").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprite_\(computer_graphics\)#cite_note-dadgum-1) Sprites can be positioned or altered by setting attributes used during the hardware composition process. The number of sprites which can be displayed per scan line is often lower than the total number of sprites a system supports. For example, the Texas Instruments [TMS9918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TMS9918 "TMS9918") chip supports 32 sprites, but only four can appear on the same scan line.

The CPUs in modern computers, video game consoles, and mobile devices are fast enough that bitmaps can be drawn into a frame buffer without special hardware assistance. Beyond that, [GPUs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPU "GPU") can render vast numbers of scaled, rotated, [anti-aliased](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-aliased "Anti-aliased"), partially translucent, very high resolution images in parallel with the CPU.

**2.5D** (basic pronunciation **two-and-a-half dimensional, two-point-five-d**) perspective refers to [gameplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gameplay "Gameplay") or movement in a [video game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game "Video game") or [virtual reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_reality "Virtual reality") environment that is restricted to a [two-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_\(mathematics\) "Plane (mathematics)") (2D) plane with little to no access to a [third dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_space "Three-dimensional space") in a space that otherwise _appears_ to be three-dimensional and is often simulated and rendered in a 3D digital environment.

This is related to but separate from pseudo-3D perspective (sometimes called three-quarter view when the environment is portrayed from an angled top-down perspective), which refers to [2D graphical projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2D_computer_graphics "2D computer graphics") and similar techniques used to cause images or scenes to simulate the appearance of being [three-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_space_\(mathematics\) "Three-dimensional space (mathematics)") (3D) when in fact they are not.

By contrast, games, spaces or perspectives that are simulated and rendered in 3D and used in 3D level design are said to be _true 3D,_ and 2D rendered games made to appear as 2D without approximating a 3D image are said to be _true 2D_.

Common in video games, 2.5D projections have also been useful in [geographic visualization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_visualization "Geographic visualization") (GVIS) to help understand visual-cognitive spatial representations or 3D visualization.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2.5D#cite_note-MacEachren-1)

The terms _three-quarter perspective_ and _three-quarter view_ trace their origins to the [three-quarter profile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-quarter_profile "Three-quarter profile") in [portraiture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portrait "Portrait") and [facial recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_perception "Face perception"), which depicts a person's face that is partway between a frontal view and a side view.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2.5D#cite_note-2)

## Computer graphics

### Axonometric and oblique projection

See also: [Isometric computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_computer_graphics "Isometric computer graphics")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4b/Lincity-ng.png/250px-Lincity-ng.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Lincity-ng.png)

_[Lincity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincity "Lincity")_ tiles 2D [axonometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_video_game_graphics "Isometric video game graphics") graphical elements to form a pseudo-3D game environment.

In [axonometric projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axonometric_projection "Axonometric projection") and [oblique projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_projection "Oblique projection"), two forms of [parallel projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_projection "Parallel projection"), the viewpoint is rotated slightly to reveal other facets of the environment than what are visible in a [top-down perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top-down_perspective "Top-down perspective") or side view, thereby producing a three-dimensional effect. An object is "considered to be in an inclined position resulting in foreshortening of all three axes",[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2.5D#cite_note-WPCleanerAuto1-3) and the image is a "representation on a single plane (as a drawing surface) of a three-dimensional object placed at an angle to the plane of projection."[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2.5D#cite_note-WPCleanerAuto1-3) Lines perpendicular to the plane become points, lines parallel to the plane have true length, and lines inclined to the plane are foreshortened.

They are popular camera perspectives among [2D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2D_computer_graphics "2D computer graphics") video games, most commonly those released for [16-bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_video_game_consoles_\(fourth_generation\) "History of video game consoles (fourth generation)") or earlier and [handheld consoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handheld_video_game "Handheld video game"), as well as in later [strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategy_video_game "Strategy video game") and [role-playing video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-playing_video_game "Role-playing video game"). The advantage of these perspectives is that they combine the visibility and mobility of a [top-down game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top-down_perspective "Top-down perspective") with the character recognizability of a [side-scrolling game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side-scrolling_video_game "Side-scrolling video game"). Thus the player can be presented an overview of the game world in the ability to see it from above, more or less, and with additional details in artwork made possible by using an angle: Instead of showing a humanoid in top-down perspective, as a head and shoulders seen from above, the entire body can be drawn when using a slanted angle; turning a character around would reveal how it looks from the sides, the front and the back, while the top-down perspective will display the same head and shoulders regardless.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ea/Sprite_anatomy_2d.svg/250px-Sprite_anatomy_2d.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sprite_anatomy_2d.svg)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Sprite_anatomy_3d.svg/250px-Sprite_anatomy_3d.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sprite_anatomy_3d.svg)

Anatomy of an axonometric sprite. 2D [sprite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprite_\(computer_graphics\) "Sprite (computer graphics)") coordinates are on the left. 3D model coordinates are on the right.

There are three main divisions of axonometric projection: _isometric_ (equal measure), _dimetric_ (symmetrical and unsymmetrical), and _trimetric_ (single-view or only two sides). The most common of these drawing types in [engineering drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering_drawing "Engineering drawing") is isometric projection. This projection is tilted so that all three axes create equal angles at intervals of 120 degrees. The result is that all three axes are equally foreshortened. In video games, a form of dimetric projection with a 2:1 pixel ratio is more common due to the problems of anti-aliasing and square pixels found on most computer monitors.

In [oblique projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_projection "Oblique projection") typically all three axes are shown without foreshortening. All lines parallel to the axes are drawn to scale, and diagonals and curved lines are distorted. One tell-tale sign of oblique projection is that the face pointed toward the camera retains its right angles with respect to the image plane.[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_]

## Benefits

In an application where many small [textures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_\(computer_graphics\) "Texture (computer graphics)") are used frequently, it is often more efficient to store the textures in a texture atlas which is treated as a single unit by the [graphics hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphics_processing_unit "Graphics processing unit"). This reduces both the disk [I/O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input/output "Input/output") overhead and the overhead of a context switch by increasing [memory locality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_locality "Memory locality"). Careful alignment may be needed to avoid bleeding between sub textures when used with [mipmapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmapping "Mipmapping") and [texture compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_compression "Texture compression").

In [web development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_development "Web development"), images are packed into a sprite sheet to reduce the number of image resources that need to be fetched in order to display a page.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_atlas#cite_note-2)


---


In [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics "Computer graphics"), a **mipmap** (_mip_ being an acronym of the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") phrase _multum in parvo_, meaning "much in little"[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-staff-1)) is a pre-calculated, [optimized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_\(computer_science\) "Optimization (computer science)") sequence of [images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_image "Digital image"), each of which has an [image resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_resolution "Image resolution") which is a factor of two smaller than the previous.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-4) Their use is known as _mipmapping_.

They are intended to increase [rendering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendering_\(computer_graphics\) "Rendering (computer graphics)") speed and reduce [aliasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliasing "Aliasing") artifacts. A high-resolution mipmap image is used for high-density samples, such as for objects close to the camera; lower-resolution images are used as the object appears farther away. This is a more efficient way of [downscaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_scaling "Image scaling") a [texture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_mapping "Texture mapping") than sampling all [texels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texel_\(graphics\) "Texel (graphics)") in the original texture that would contribute to a screen [pixel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel "Pixel"); it is faster to take a constant number of samples from the appropriately downfiltered textures. Since mipmaps, by definition, are [pre-allocated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_management "Memory management"), additional [storage space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_data_storage "Computer data storage") is required to take advantage of them. They are also related to [wavelet compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelet_compression "Wavelet compression").

Mipmaps are widely used in 3D [computer games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_game "Computer game"), [flight simulators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_simulator "Flight simulator"), other 3D imaging systems for [texture filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_filtering "Texture filtering"), and 2D and 3D [GIS software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_information_system "Geographic information system"). Mipmap textures are used in 3D scenes to decrease the time required to render a scene. They also improve [image quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_quality "Image quality") by reducing aliasing and [Moiré patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moir%C3%A9_pattern "Moiré pattern") that occur at large viewing distances,[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-5) at the cost of [33%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1/4_%2B_1/16_%2B_1/64_%2B_1/256_%2B_%E2%8B%AF "1/4 + 1/16 + 1/64 + 1/256 + ⋯") more memory per texture.


---


The origin of the term mipmap is an initialism of the Latin phrase _multum in parvo_ ("much in little"), and map, modeled on bitmap.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-staff-1) The term _pyramids_ is still commonly used in a [GIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_information_system "Geographic information system") context. In GIS software, pyramids are primarily used for speeding up rendering times.

| ## **E. The Oct-Tet Algorithm**<br><br>The first line of code sets point(0,0,0) equal to 1 by definition. Next we assign a value of 1 to any point (on a **vertex**) that has 2 coordinates of zero. If an element is not the initial point or a vertex point then we check to see if it's on the tetrahedral face. If it is we sum the two values from the level above for any element that is on the **edge** of a level. The final ELSE statement assigns a value for all the points **not on an edge or vertex**, setting them equal to the sum of the 3 elements from the level above.<br><br>Let PT(0,0,0) = 1 <br>    FOR level = 1 TO top <br>        FOR x = level TO 0 STEP -1 <br>            FOR y = (level - x) TO 0 STEP -1 <br>                FOR z = (level - (x+y)) TO 0 STEP -1 <br>                IF x = 0 AND y = 0 OR x = 0 AND z = 0 <br>                OR y = 0 AND z = 0 THEN <br>                    LET pt(x,y,z) = 1 <br>                ELSE IF x = 0 <br>                    LET pt(x,y,z) = pt(x,y-1,z) + pt(z,y,z-1) <br>                ELSE IF y = 0 THEN <br>                    LET pt(x,y,z) = pt(x-1,y,z) + pt(z,y,z-1) <br>                ELSE IF x = 0 THEN <br>                    LET pt(x,y,z) = When working with libGDX (or any other OpenGL based system), you will have to deal with various [coordinate systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_system). This is because OpenGL abstracts away device dependent units, making it more convenient to target multiple devices and to focus on game logic. Sometimes you may need to convert between coordinate systems for which libGDX offers various methods.

**It is crucial to understand in which coordinate system you are working. Otherwise it can be easy to get confused and to make assumptions which aren’t correct.**

On this page the various coordinate systems are listed. It is highly recommended to first get familiar with the concept of [Cartesian coordinate systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate_system), which is the most widely used one.

- [Touch coordinates](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#touch-coordinates)
- [Screen or image coordinates](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#screen-or-image-coordinates)
- [Pixmap and texture coordinates](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#pixmap-and-texture-coordinates)
- [Normalized render coordinates](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#normalized-render-coordinates)
- [Normalized texture (UV) coordinates](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#normalized-texture-UV-coordinates)
- [World coordinates](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#world-coordinates)
- [GUI/HUD coordinates](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#guihud-coordinates)
- [Game coordinates](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#game-coordinates)

## Touch coordinates[Permalink](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#touch-coordinates "Permalink")

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Units|pixels|
|System|y-down|
|Type|integer (can't be fractional)|
|Range|(`0`, `0`) (upper left corner) to (`Gdx.graphics.getWidth()-1`, `Gdx.graphics.getHeight()-1`) (lower right corner)|
|Usage|touch/mouse coordinates|
|Dependence|device specific|

Starts at the upper left _pixel_ of the (application portion of the) physical screen and has the size of the (application portion of the) physical screens width and height in pixels.

![images/screenpixels.png](https://libgdx.com/assets/wiki/images/screenpixels.png)

Each coordinate is an index in the 2D array of this grid, representing a physical pixel on the screen. Therefore these coordinates are always represented as integers, they can’t be fractional.

This coordinate system is based on the classic representation of the display, which is usually also closest to the device/OS specific implementation. If you’re familiar with canvas graphics or basic image editors, then you are probably already familiar with these coordinates. You might even lean towards using these as your default/favorite, which you shouldn’t.

Whenever working with [mouse or touch](https://libgdx.com/wiki/input/mouse-touch-and-keyboard) coordinates, you’ll be using this coordinate system. You typically want to convert these coordinates as soon as possible to a more convenient coordinate system. E.g. the `camera.unproject` or `viewport.unproject` method let’s you convert them to world coordinates (see below).

## Screen or image coordinates[Permalink](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#screen-or-image-coordinates "Permalink")

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Units|pixels|
|System|y-up|
|Type|integer (can't be fractional)|
|Range|(`0`, `0`) (lower left corner) to (`Gdx.graphics.getWidth()-1`, `Gdx.graphics.getHeight()-1`) (upper right corner)|
|Usage|viewport, scissors and pixmap|
|Dependence|device/resource/asset specific|

This is OpenGL’s counterpart to touch coordinates; that is: it is used to specify (index) a pixel of the (portion of the) physical screen. It is also used as indexer for an image in memory. Likewise, these are integers, they can’t be fractional.

The only difference between touch and screen coordinates is that touch coordinates are y-down, while screen coordinates are y-up. Converting between them is therefore quite easy:

Copy code`y = Gdx.graphics.getHeight() - 1 - y;`

You typically use these coordinates to specify which portion of the screen to render onto. For example when calling [`glViewport`](https://www.khronos.org/opengles/sdk/1.1/docs/man/glViewport.xml), [`glScissor`](https://www.khronos.org/opengles/sdk/1.1/docs/man/glScissor.xml) or manipulating a pixmap (see next). In the majority of use-cases you don’t need this coordinate system a lot, if any, and it should be isolated from your game logic and its coordinate system. The `camera.project` and `viewport.project` methods can be used to convert world units to screen coordinates.

### Pixmap and texture coordinates[Permalink](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#pixmap-and-texture-coordinates "Permalink")

Pixmap coordinates are an exception. Pixmaps are commonly used to upload texture data. For example when loading a PNG image file to a texture, it is first decoded (uncompressed) to a Pixmap, which is the raw pixel data of the image, then it is copied to the GPU for use as texture. The texture can then be used to render to the screen. It is also possible to modify or create a pixmap by code, e.g. before uploading as texture data.

The “problem” with this is that OpenGL expects the texture data to be in image coordinates, which is y-up. However, most image formats store the image data comparable to touch coordinates, which is y-down. libGDX does not translate the image data between the two (which would involve copying the image line by line), instead it simply copies the data as is. This practically causes a Texture loaded from Pixmap to be up-side-down.

To compensate for this up-side-down texture, `SpriteBatch` flips the texture (UV) coordinates (see below) on the y axis when rendering. Likewise, fbx-conv has the option to flip texture coordinates on the y axis as well. However, when you use a texture which isn’t loaded from a pixmap, for example a Framebuffer, then this might cause that texture to appear up-side-down.

## Normalized render coordinates[Permalink](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#normalized-render-coordinates "Permalink")

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Units|one|
|System|y-up|
|Type|floating point|
|Range|(`-1`, `-1`) (lower left corner) to (`+1`, `+1`) (upper right corner)|
|Usage|shaders|
|Dependence|none|

The above coordinate systems have one big issue in common: they are device specific. To solve that, OpenGL allows you to use a device independent coordinate system which is automatically mapped to screen coordinates when rendering. This coordinate system is normalized in the range [-1,-1] and [+1,+1] with (0,0) exactly in the center of the screen or framebuffer (the render target).

![images/normalizedcoordinates.png](https://libgdx.com/assets/wiki/images/normalizedcoordinates.png)

The _vertex shader_ outputs (`gl_Position`) its coordinates in this coordinate system. But other than that, you should never have to use this coordinate system in a practical use-case. It is sometimes used in tutorials and such, though, to show the basics.

It might be worth to note that the normalization does not respect the aspect ratio. That is: the scale in the X direction does not have to match the scale in the Y direction. They are both within the range of `-1` to `+1`, regardless aspect ratio. It is up to the application to decide how to deal with various aspect ratios (see world units, below).

The coordinates are floating point and no longer indexers. The device (GPU) will map these coordinates to the actual screen pixels using [rasterisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasterisation). A good article (although targeting DirectX it also applies to OpenGL) for more information on that can be found [here](https://msdn.microsoft.com/en-us/library/windows/desktop/cc627092\(v=vs.85\).aspx).

## Normalized texture (UV) coordinates[Permalink](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#normalized-texture-uv-coordinates "Permalink")

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Units|one|
|System|y-up|
|Type|floating point|
|Range|(`0`, `0`) (lower left corner) to (`1`, `1`) (upper right corner)|
|Usage|shaders, mesh, texture region, sprite|
|Dependence|none|

Likewise to the normalized render coordinates, OpenGL also uses normalized texture coordinates. The only difference is that these ranges from [0,0] to [1,1]. Depending on the specified wrap function, values outside that range will be mapped within that range.

![images/texturecoordinates.png](https://libgdx.com/assets/wiki/images/texturecoordinates.png)

These coordinates are also called **UV coordinates**. In many use cases you don’t have to deal with them. Typically these values are stored in the mesh or `TextureRegion`.

The use of normalized texture coordinates is very important, because it makes them independent of the asset size. Or in other words: it allows you to replace your assets with a scaled down or scaled up version, without having to modify the UV coordinates. An example where this is used are [mipmaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap).

When rendering, the GPU converts the UV coordinates to a [texel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texel_\(graphics\)) (texture pixel). This is called “texture sampling” and is based on the [texture filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_filtering).

## World coordinates[Permalink](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#world-coordinates "Permalink")

Typically, your game logic should use a coordinate system which best fits the game logic. It should not depend on device or asset size. For example, a commonly used unit is meters.

The world coordinates are converted, in the vertex shader, to normalized render coordinates. The [Camera](https://web.archive.org/web/20200427232345/https://www.badlogicgames.com/wordpress/?p=1550) or [Viewport](https://libgdx.com/wiki/graphics/viewports) is used to define the strategy on how to do that. For example, to maintain aspect ratio, black bars can be added. The camera is used to calculate the view matrix, which translates your world coordinates into coordinates relative to the camera, by taking in consideration the location and rotation of the camera. It also calculates the projection matrix, which converts the world coordinates to the normalized render coordinates in the range [-1,-1] to [+1,+1]. In 2D games, you mostly dont need the distinction between these two matrices and only need the combined transformation matrix instead. You can pass this matrix to the shader, for example, by calling `spriteBatch.setProjectionMatrix(camera.combined);`

![images/projection.png](https://libgdx.com/assets/wiki/images/projection.png)

You can have multiple camera’s or viewports and likewise, you can also have multiple world coordinate systems. A typical game has at least two of those, namely:

### GUI/HUD coordinates[Permalink](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#guihud-coordinates "Permalink")

These are buttons, labels and such which are stationary and always visible on the screen. Often they involve rendering text. For example in Super Mario the clock is always visible in the upper right corner of the screen and does not move when mario moves in the game world.

Most commonly [scene2d](https://libgdx.com/wiki/graphics/2d/scene2d/scene2d) is used for the HUD, which means that you’d use a [Viewport](https://libgdx.com/wiki/graphics/viewports) to define the coordinate system. This coordinates system is typically in a range that is close to the device resolution, to give the best results when rendering the font. These coordinates are called [banana units](https://xoppa.github.io/blog/pixels/). This camera is practically never moved or rotated, it sits stationary at a location so that the world coordinate (0, 0) is located at the bottom left corner of the screen.

### Game coordinates[Permalink](https://libgdx.com/wiki/articles/coordinate-systems#game-coordinates "Permalink")

This is what suits best for your game and is used to implement game logic. It is good practice to keep the values around one for the best floating point precision. For example, your main character is 1.8 meter in height, the tree is 10 meter in height, etc. If you are making a galactic game where units and distance are very high, you might want to use e.g. kilometers instead.

The camera is used to look into your game world, just like it would when you use a video camera in the real world. The camera can be moved, rotated and scaled to display another portion of the world on the screen.

The origin of the term mipmap is an initialism of the Latin phrase _multum in parvo_ ("much in little"), and map, modeled on bitmap.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-staff-1) The term _pyramids_ is still commonly used in a [GIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_information_system "Geographic information system") context. In GIS software, pyramids are primarily used for speeding up rendering times.

## Mechanism

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5c/MipMap_Example_STS101.jpg/250px-MipMap_Example_STS101.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:MipMap_Example_STS101.jpg)

Example mipmap image storage: the principal image on the left is accompanied by filtered copies of reduced size.

Each bitmap image of the mipmap set is a downsized duplicate of the main [texture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_mapping "Texture mapping"), but at a certain reduced level of detail. Although the main texture would still be used when the view is sufficient to render it in full detail, the renderer will switch to a suitable mipmap image (or in fact, [interpolate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpolation_\(computer_graphics\) "Interpolation (computer graphics)") between the two nearest, if [trilinear filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trilinear_filtering "Trilinear filtering") is activated) when the texture is viewed from a distance or at a small size. Rendering speed increases since the number of texture pixels (_[texels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texel_\(graphics\) "Texel (graphics)")_) being processed per display pixel can be much lower for similar results with the simpler mipmap textures. If using a limited number of texture samples per display pixel (as is the case with [bilinear filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_filtering "Bilinear filtering")) then artifacts are reduced since the mipmap images are effectively already [anti-aliased](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_anti-aliasing "Spatial anti-aliasing"). Scaling down and up is made more efficient with mipmaps as well.

If the texture has a basic size of 256 by 256 pixels, then the associated mipmap set may contain a series of 8 images, each one-fourth the total area of the previous one: 128×128 pixels, 64×64, 32×32, 16×16, 8×8, 4×4, 2×2, 1×1 (a single pixel). If, for example, a scene is rendering this texture in a space of 40×40 pixels, then either a scaled-up version of the 32×32 (without [trilinear interpolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trilinear_interpolation "Trilinear interpolation")) or an interpolation of the 64×64 and the 32×32 mipmaps (with trilinear interpolation) would be used. The simplest way to generate these textures is by successive averaging; however, more sophisticated algorithms (perhaps based on [signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_processing "Signal processing") and [Fourier transforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform")) can also be used.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ed/Mipmap_illustration2.png/250px-Mipmap_illustration2.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mipmap_illustration2.png)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7f/Mipmap_illustration1.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mipmap_illustration1.png)

Showing each color channel of each level of an RGB mipmap as a separate plane (left) demonstrates that the whole mipmap forms a square of 4 times the area. As each plane requires 1⁄3 the storage, mipmaps therefore require 4⁄3 the memory; i.e., 1⁄3 ≈ 33% more.

The increase in storage space required for all of these mipmaps is a third of the original texture, because the sum of the areas [1/4 + 1/16 + 1/64 + 1/256 + ⋯](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1/4_%2B_1/16_%2B_1/64_%2B_1/256_%2B_%E2%8B%AF "1/4 + 1/16 + 1/64 + 1/256 + ⋯") converges to 1/3. In the case of an RGB image with three channels stored as separate planes, the total mipmap can be visualized as fitting neatly into a square area twice as large as the dimensions of the original image on each side (twice as large on each side is four times the original area - one plane of the original size for each of red, green and blue makes three times the original area, and then since the smaller textures take 1/3 of the original, 1/3 of three is one, so they will take the same total space as just one of the original red, green, or blue planes). This is the inspiration for the tag _multum in parvo_.

## Uses

[![Image showing how mipmaps reduce aliasing at large distances.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Mipmap_Aliasing_Comparison.png/500px-Mipmap_Aliasing_Comparison.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mipmap_Aliasing_Comparison.png)

Image showing how mipmaps reduce [aliasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliasing "Aliasing") at large distances (Aliasing causes a [Moiré pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moir%C3%A9_pattern "Moiré pattern") in the left image.)

Mipmaps are used for:

- [Level of detail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Level_of_detail_\(computer_graphics\) "Level of detail (computer graphics)") (LOD)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-8)
- Improving image quality. Rendering from large textures where only small, discontiguous subsets of [texels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texel_\(graphics\) "Texel (graphics)") are used can easily produce [Moiré patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moir%C3%A9_pattern "Moiré pattern");
- Speeding up rendering times, either by reducing the number of texels sampled to render each pixel, or increasing the memory locality of the samples taken;
- Reducing stress on the [GPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPU "GPU") or [CPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPU "CPU").
- Water surface reflections[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-9)

## Anisotropic filtering

Main article: [Anisotropic filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropic_filtering "Anisotropic filtering")

When a texture is viewed at a steep angle, the filtering should not be uniform in each direction (it should be [anisotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropic "Anisotropic") rather than [isotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropic "Isotropic")), and a compromise resolution is required. If a higher resolution is used, the [cache coherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cache_coherence "Cache coherence") goes down, and the aliasing is increased in one direction, but the image tends to be clearer. If a lower resolution is used, the cache coherence is improved, but the image is overly blurry. This would be a tradeoff of MIP level of detail (LOD) for aliasing vs blurriness. However anisotropic filtering attempts to resolve this trade-off by sampling a non isotropic texture footprint for each pixel rather than merely adjusting the MIP LOD. This non isotropic texture sampling requires either a more sophisticated storage scheme or a summation of more texture fetches at higher frequencies.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap#cite_note-10)
